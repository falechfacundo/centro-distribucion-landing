OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) help a team point in the same direction. But poorly written, they turn into a task list in disguise. This guide shows you how to define them well.

## The structure

An OKR has two parts:

- **Objective (O):** what you want to achieve. It is qualitative, inspiring and time-bound.
- **Key results (KR):** how you will know you achieved it. They are measurable and have a number.

An objective usually has between two and five key results.

## Rules for a good objective

1. **It is clear and memorable.** If it is hard to explain in one sentence, it is poorly framed.
2. **It is ambitious but possible.** It should make you a little uncomfortable.
3. **It has a horizon.** Usually a quarter.
4. **It describes an outcome, not an activity.** "Launch the app" is a task; "Teams adopt the app" is an objective.

## Rules for good key results

- **Measurable:** they have a starting value and a target value.
- **About outcomes, not tasks:** "Publish 10 articles" is an activity; "Reach 5,000 monthly visits" is an outcome.
- **Verifiable:** anyone can look at the number and say whether it was met.

## Example

**Objective:** New teams start working in the product from day one.

- Cut onboarding time from 30 to 5 minutes.
- Raise new-team activation from 40% to 65%.
- Reach an onboarding NPS above 50.

## Common mistakes

- **Too many OKRs.** With more than three or four objectives per team, focus is lost.
- **Key results that are tasks.** If you can cross it off a list, it is probably not a KR.
- **Objectives that cannot be measured.** Without numbers there is no way to know whether you progressed.
- **Writing them and forgetting them.** Review progress every week or two.

## Connect OKRs to the work

OKRs lose strength when they live in a separate spreadsheet. Ideally you link each objective to the projects where the work happens and review progress where the team already works.
